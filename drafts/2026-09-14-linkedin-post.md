# LinkedIn Post Draft — 2026-09-14

**Pillar:** Pillar 1 — Honest Takes on Wellness Content
**Angle:** Wellness content written to pass brand review is written to be ignored — brands that sanitize the real sources of workplace stress signal they don't understand the problem they're selling a solution to.

---

Corporate wellness content is written to be approved, not believed.

Most wellness brands won't name the real sources of workplace stress — toxic management, unsustainable workloads, structural burnout — because doing so feels like validating employee grievances instead of solving problems. So the content stays safe: breathing tips, resilience frameworks, hydration reminders. It clears legal review. It doesn't embarrass anyone in leadership.

It also doesn't convince anyone.

HR leaders evaluating wellness platforms aren't looking for generic advice. They're deciding whether a vendor actually understands the environment they're operating in — return-to-office friction, benefits that go unused, frontline manager accountability. Content that avoids naming those problems is a signal: this platform doesn't know your world.

The wellness brands with the most credible content name the uncomfortable things. They publish on what happens when wellness programs fail. They don't sanitize the research on toxic cultures. That specificity is what earns trust — not curated positivity designed to offend no one.

If your wellness content wouldn't make anyone in a C-suite slightly uncomfortable, it probably won't move anyone in a C-suite to act.

What's the last piece of wellness content you read that actually named something real?
