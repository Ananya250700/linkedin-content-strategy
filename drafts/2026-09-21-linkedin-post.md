# LinkedIn Post Draft — 2026-09-21

**Pillar:** Pillar 1 — Honest Takes on Wellness Content
**Angle:** Wellness content is written to be approved by internal stakeholders, not to be useful to HR leaders — and that optimization is a trust signal in the wrong direction.

---

Most wellness content is written to be approved, not to be useful.

Internal stakeholders want messaging that's positive, aspirational, and safe. Legal wants liability removed. Brand wants the tone consistent. Leadership wants the company to look like a good place to work.

So that's what gets published: polished, optimistic content about wellness programs that sounds good in a board deck.

The problem: HR leaders evaluating a wellness platform aren't reading for reassurance. They're trying to solve specific, uncomfortable problems — low participation rates, leadership skepticism, workforce burnout that existing programs clearly didn't fix. They're looking for content that acknowledges what's hard, not content that pretends the situation is already good.

The tell is in the language. If a wellness vendor's blog never uses words like "low adoption," "failed rollout," or "skeptical workforce" — if it avoids any data that reflects poorly on wellness programs as a category — it's been optimized for brand safety, not for the reader.

That optimization is a trust signal in the wrong direction. It tells HR leaders this vendor doesn't understand (or won't acknowledge) the real problem.

The most useful thing wellness content can do is name the thing HR leaders know is true but nobody else will say.

What's the hardest truth your wellness content currently avoids?
